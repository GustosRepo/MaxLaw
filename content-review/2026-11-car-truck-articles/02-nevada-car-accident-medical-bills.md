# Who Pays Medical Bills After a Nevada Car Accident?

The driver who caused a crash may be responsible for your injury losses, but that does not mean the driver’s insurer will pay each medical bill as it arrives. While the claim is being evaluated, payment may involve your health insurance, purchased medical payments coverage, a provider arrangement or your own funds. Which options apply depends on the available benefits and the facts of the claim.

If the hospital sends a bill while the auto insurer is still investigating, call the provider’s billing office and ask which insurer has received the bill, whether it is pending or denied, and what amount the provider says you owe now. Ask for an itemized statement and compare it with any explanation of benefits from your insurer.

## Separate treatment bills from the injury claim

There are two questions to address: how a particular bill will be handled now, and what crash-related losses you may later recover. An insurer reviewing liability may still be investigating the collision, the treatment and the available coverage when a provider sends a bill.

You should not assume that giving a clinic an auto-insurance claim number means payment has been accepted. Ask whether the provider submitted a claim, to whom, and what response it received. Keep the bill and any explanation of benefits or denial together so you can see what remains unresolved.

This approach is useful after a crash anywhere in the valley, including Las Vegas, Henderson and North Las Vegas. If you received care in Clark County and continued treatment after returning home, keep records from both places in the same file.

## What payment sources should you check?

The following are possibilities to investigate, not a promise that every source is available or that they pay in a fixed order.

| Possible source | Documents to look for | Question to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Health insurance | Plan information, provider bill and explanation of benefits | Was the claim submitted, and what benefits, deductibles or network rules apply? |
| Medical payments coverage, or MedPay | Auto-policy declarations, coverage form and claim correspondence | Did I purchase this benefit, who is insured and how much remains available? |
| Uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage | Auto policy, endorsements and any rejection documents | Could this coverage apply to my injuries if the responsible driver lacks sufficient coverage? |
| Provider payment arrangement | Written billing, payment-plan or other agreement | What must I pay, when is it due and what happens if the injury claim does not produce a recovery? |
| Liability recovery | Claim correspondence and any proposed agreement | Has responsibility and coverage been established, and which losses does a proposed payment address? |

### Health insurance

If you have a health plan, ask the provider and plan how to submit the accident-related treatment. Check whether a denial concerns missing information, coverage, a provider-network issue or another reason. An explanation of benefits can help you compare what was billed, what the plan allowed and what it says you may owe.

Do not assume a bill is correct simply because it arrived, or that you can disregard it because someone else caused the crash. Ask for an itemized statement and an explanation of disputed charges. Keep notes of calls and copies of any appeal or information request.

### Nevada MedPay coverage

[NRS 687B.145(3)](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-687B.html#NRS687BSec145) requires insurers transacting motor-vehicle insurance in Nevada to offer an insured with a passenger-car or motorcycle policy the option to buy at least $1,000 of medical-expense coverage. A required offer does not mean you purchased the benefit.

The Nevada Division of Insurance explains that MedPay can cover qualifying medical expenses without regard to fault. If you bought it, review the actual limits, insured-person definitions and claim requirements rather than assuming every treatment bill will be paid. [Nevada DOI consumer guide, medical-payments discussion](https://doi.nv.gov/uploadedFiles/doi.nv.gov/Content/Consumers/Automobile_Insurance/2024_Auto_Guide_FINAL.pdf).

### What if the driver who caused the crash is uninsured?

Review your own auto coverage as well as any information about the other driver. Nevada’s [UM/UIM offer rules](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-687B.html#NRS687BSec145) and [uninsured-vehicle coverage provisions](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-690B.html#NRS690BSec020) do not mean every injured person has purchased coverage for every situation.

UM/UIM is also not simply a replacement health plan that automatically pays bills on receipt. Coverage, legal responsibility, injury losses and policy conditions may need to be evaluated. Max Law’s [uninsured and underinsured accident page](https://www.maxlawnv.com/practice/underinsured-and-uninsured-accidents) explains the broader coverage-review issue.

## What if you do not have health insurance?

Ask about purchased MedPay and any other potentially applicable benefits. You can also ask the provider about financial assistance, payment options or a written billing arrangement. Get the terms before assuming payment can wait until the case ends.

If a provider proposes payment from a future recovery, ask what happens if the claim is unsuccessful or the recovery does not cover the balance. A treatment arrangement does not guarantee compensation, erase a debt or establish that every charge is recoverable from another party.

If you are injured, seek appropriate medical care and raise payment concerns with the provider. An attorney can discuss claim and coverage questions; that conversation is not a substitute for medical evaluation or a promise to fund treatment.

## Can someone seek repayment after paying a bill?

Payment and final responsibility are not always the same. Some benefits or arrangements may involve a later reimbursement claim. A medical bill, a claimed lien and an insurer’s repayment request should be examined separately.

Medicare is one example with its own federal process. Under Medicare Secondary Payer rules, Medicare may make a conditional payment when a responsible primary plan does not pay promptly. It may seek repayment when there is a settlement or other qualifying payment. That process should not be treated as the rule for every private health plan. [CMS conditional-payment guidance, 42 U.S.C. § 1395y(b)](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coordination-benefits-recovery/attorney-services/conditional-payment-information).

Tell your attorney about benefits that paid for care and any repayment correspondence. For questions about deductions and money distributed after a case resolves, see Max Law’s existing [insurance settlements and checks guide](https://www.maxlawnv.com/practice/insurance-settlements-and-checks).

## A simple way to track bills while your claim is pending

Create a list with one row for each provider or account. Record the treatment date, amount billed, insurer contacted, response, payments and the balance being questioned. Keep related documents together rather than forwarding a bill without its explanation of benefits.

If you receive separate statements from an emergency department, radiology provider or ambulance service, log each under its own account number. A payment or denial for one statement may leave the others outstanding. Ask each billing office what it has on file before assuming every charge went through the same insurer.

Useful questions include:

- Has this bill been submitted to the intended insurer?
- Is more information needed, and who needs to provide it?
- Is the charge disputed, denied or still being processed?
- Is a payment arrangement in writing, and what are its conditions?
- Has anyone asserted a right to repayment from a recovery?

If a collection notice arrives, address it promptly with the provider and your attorney rather than assuming the accident claim stops the process. Do not send sensitive medical records through an unsecured public contact form; ask how to provide them securely.
