# What Evidence Matters After a Las Vegas Truck Accident?

A truck accident claim may depend on evidence that never appears in the police report: driver logs, electronic records, maintenance files, dispatch messages and the condition of the truck itself. Different people may control those materials, and they may not keep them for the same length of time.

If you were injured, save the records you already have and ask promptly about preserving other evidence. You do not need access to the trucking company’s files before speaking with an attorney.

## Start with what you can safely document

Your own records can help establish where the collision happened, who was involved and what changed afterward. Keep original photographs and videos, witness contact details, the incident number, insurance correspondence and treatment records. Save repair estimates and information showing where the damaged vehicle is being stored.

If you have a photo of the truck, keep the full image. A company name, vehicle number, plate or USDOT number may help identify participants. The tractor, trailer and cargo can have different owners or business connections; one logo does not explain every relationship.

For a crash on I-15, US-95 or the Beltway, note the direction of travel, nearby interchange and approximate time. For a collision on a Las Vegas, Henderson or North Las Vegas street, preserve the street names and any nearby business or camera locations. Do not return to traffic or enter private property to gather evidence.

## Which records may be held by someone else?

The evidence needed depends on the issues being investigated. A brake-failure allegation raises different questions from a lane-change dispute or a possible fatigue-related crash.

| Evidence | Possible holder | What it may help investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Driver duty logs and supporting trip records | Carrier, driver or service provider | Driving and rest periods, trip sequence and inconsistencies between records |
| Dispatch messages and delivery information | Carrier, dispatcher or logistics provider | Instructions, schedules and the work being performed |
| Electronic vehicle data | Vehicle owner, carrier, equipment or technology provider | Available information about operation or a recorded event |
| Maintenance, repair and inspection records | Carrier, repair business or equipment owner | Reported defects, repairs and the vehicle’s condition |
| Loading records and photographs | Shipper, warehouse, loader or carrier | Cargo condition, loading and securement questions |
| Video | Fleet operator, motorist, business or public agency | Vehicle movements or conditions visible within the recording |
| Driver qualification information | Carrier and relevant record holders | Training, licensing and qualification questions |

These are possible sources, not a statement that every record exists or proves wrongdoing. An investigation should compare records with the collision evidence and the injuries being claimed.

## ELD records, vehicle event data and video are different

### Electronic logging devices

An electronic logging device, or ELD, is used to record driver duty-status information where the rules require it. Those records may help investigate driving time and rest periods. They are not a continuous video of the trip, and they do not answer every question about what happened at impact.

Federal logging requirements have exceptions. The applicable operation and records must be identified before concluding that a missing ELD record shows a violation. [49 CFR 395.8](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-395/subpart-A/section-395.8).

### ECM and event information

An engine control module, or ECM, and any available event-recording functions are separate from the ELD’s duty-status purpose. What can be recovered depends on the vehicle, equipment, event and available data. An expert may need to identify the relevant system and appropriate download method.

There is no sound reason to assume every truck has the same “black box,” records the same information or overwrites it after a universal number of days.

### Cameras in the Las Vegas Valley

Identify a camera’s owner before assuming footage exists. A fleet camera, a motorist’s dashcam and a business security camera are different potential sources.

[Nevada 511’s official FAQ](https://www.nvroads.com/about/faq) says its traffic-monitoring cameras do **not** record and that footage from those cameras is unavailable. Seeing a freeway camera does not mean there is a saved video of your collision. Other camera systems require their own investigation.

## How long must trucking records be kept?

Federal rules prescribe retention periods for particular records in covered operations. Nevada also adopts motor-carrier safety regulations with state-specific provisions; applicability must be checked for the vehicle and trip. [NAC 706.2472](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NAC/NAC-706.html#NAC706Sec2472).

| Record category | Rule to examine | What the rule covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duty-status records and required supporting documents | [49 CFR 395.8(k)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-395/subpart-A/section-395.8) | Carrier retention for at least six months from receipt |
| ELD backup copy | [49 CFR 395.22(i)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-395/subpart-B/section-395.22) | Six-month backup on a separate device |
| Records required by the inspection/maintenance section | [49 CFR 396.3(b)–(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-396/section-396.3) | Specified records generally concern vehicles controlled for 30 consecutive days. Section .3(c) specifies a one-year retention period and retention for six months after the vehicle leaves carrier control |
| Accident register and specified accident reports | [49 CFR 390.15(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-390/subpart-B/section-390.15) | Three years after each covered accident |

These rules do not establish a single deadline for every piece of crash evidence. The accident-register requirement, for example, does not mean all camera or electronic vehicle data must remain available for three years. Nor is a regulatory retention minimum permission to destroy information that must be preserved for litigation.

## Preserving evidence and obtaining it are separate steps

An attorney may identify record holders, request preservation and evaluate whether an inspection or formal legal process is appropriate. A preservation request can identify relevant material and explain why it matters. It is not a court order, and it does not automatically give the requester possession of the records.

In a Nevada state-court case, [Rules 34 and 45 of the Nevada Rules of Civil Procedure](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/CourtRules/NRCP.html) provide procedures for document production, inspections and subpoenas, subject to their requirements and protections. Other courts may apply different procedural rules.

Keep evidence you control, too. Preserve original messages, files and recordings rather than relying only on edited clips. If a vehicle may be repaired, sold or disposed of, raise that with counsel promptly so inspection and preservation options can be considered. Do not access another person’s accounts, equipment or property without lawful authority.

## What if evidence has already been lost?

Loss of evidence does not automatically decide a case. For electronically stored information, [Nevada Rule 37(e)](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/CourtRules/NRCP.html) addresses information that should have been preserved for anticipated or ongoing litigation but was lost because a party failed to take reasonable preservation steps, and cannot be restored or replaced. Remedies depend on the rule’s findings; the most serious specified sanctions require intent to deprive another party of its use.

Other copies, witness accounts or related records may still help. Tell your attorney what you believe is missing and why, without assuming the loss proves the crash claim or guarantees a sanction.
